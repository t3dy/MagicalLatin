Latin Quotation

Tu autem non debes praecipere ut ferum adferat, quia Goetia non ferrum, sed alia metalla quae nominata sunt, in suis operationibus adhibet.
Translation: "However, you should not command that he bring iron, for the Goetia do not use iron, but other metals which are named, in their operations."

Source Information

Original Text: Unknown Source

Cited by Scholar: Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Work: The Goetia of Dr. Rudd

Scholarly Commentary

⌘ Historical Context
Skinner and Rankine's edition of Dr. Rudd's work demonstrates how Goetic practice modified planetary correspondence theory. The metal prohibitions reveal a practical magical philosophy where theoretical correspondence (Mars = iron) is overridden by experiential constraint (spirits 'cannot abide' iron). This negotiation between theory and practice characterizes Renaissance grimoire philosophy and shows how magical systems evolved through practical limitation.
— Stephen Skinner and David Rankine
⌘ Linguistic Analysis
Medieval Latin construction with conditional imperative (ne praecipies). The phrase 'Goetia non ferrum' uses metonymy (the art name standing for practitioners). 'Quae nominata sunt' (which are named) refers to previous enumeration of planetary metals. Classical constructions adapted for practical magical instruction.
— Stephen Skinner and David Rankine
⌘ Magical Significance
Reveals the hierarchical constraint system of Goetic magic: spirits are bound by rules of elemental and planetary affinity. Iron's prohibition demonstrates that Mars-ruled spirits have specific incompatibilities—likely connected to concepts of binding and constraint in Goetic philosophy where iron is traditionally binding/constraining, paradoxically excluding precisely the metal associated with their ruling planet. This shows practitioners understood contradictions in correspondence theory.
— Stephen Skinner and David Rankine
